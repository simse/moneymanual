# Writing style guide

Distilled from the GOV.UK style guide and the AGO Writing Style Guide. Use as a reusable reference for all articles.

## Six principles of good writing

1. Know your audience. Write as if speaking to one specific reader. Consider their workload, motivation and understanding.
2. Focus on your purpose. Sum up your reason for writing in one sentence and put it near the top.
3. Plan a logical structure. List your points first, then organise them around the audience and purpose.
4. Write clearly in plain English. Everyday words, short active sentences, sub-headings.
5. Set the right tone: open, positive, human, polite.
6. Edit and proofread. Read aloud, cut what you do not need, and ask someone unfamiliar with the topic to check it.

## Plain English

Use everyday words. Prefer "buy" over "purchase", "help" over "assist", "about" over "approximately", "like" over "such as".

Aim for 15 to 20 words per sentence; check anything over 25. One idea per paragraph. Break up text with sub-headings.

Use the active voice: "the team made a decision", not "a decision was made by the team".

Use verbs, not noun forms of verbs. "We will discuss this" beats "We will have a discussion about this".

Explain abbreviations in full at first mention, then use the short form. You do not need to explain widely-known ones (BBC, NHS, EU, UK, USA, MP, MOT, file types like PDF).

Avoid jargon, Latin and legalese. If a legal term is unavoidable, define it in plain English. Avoid "eg", "ie" and "etc" — use "for example", "such as", "like", "including" or rewrite. Spell out "per cent" but use the % symbol with a number.

Avoid negative contractions ("can't", "don't") — use "cannot", "do not". Skip complex contractions ("should've").

## Words to avoid

agenda (use "plan"), advance (use "improve"), collaborate (use "work with"), combat (use "fix"), commit/pledge (be specific), counter (use "prevent"), deliver (use "make", "create", "provide"), deploy (use "use"), dialogue (use "discussion"), empower (use "allow"), facilitate (be specific), focus (use "work on"), foster (use "encourage"), impact as a verb (use "affect"), incentivise (use "encourage"), initiate (use "start"), key (use "important"), land as a verb (use "achieve"), leverage (use "use"), liaise (use "work with"), overarching (usually superfluous), progress as a verb (use "develop"), promote (use "recommend"), robust (use "well thought out"), slim down (use "reduce"), streamline (use "simplify"), strengthen (be specific), tackle (use "deal with"), transform (describe the change), utilise (use "use").

Avoid metaphors: drive, going forward, hub, portal, one-stop shop, in order to, ring-fencing.

## Tone

Tell the reader what they can do, not what they cannot. Positive language ("can", "will") sounds helpful; negative language ("not possible") sounds aggressive.

Address the reader as "you". Avoid gendered pronouns — use "they", "their", "them". When you must refer to gender, use "women" and "men", not "males" and "females".

There is usually no need to write "please" or "please note". Use direct commands for instructions: "follow these steps".

## Formatting

Use sentence case for everything, including titles and headings. Capitalise only proper nouns: specific government departments and roles ("Minister for Housing"), named programmes, place names, brand names, titles of acts, full job titles tied to a name. Use lower case for generic terms: government, minister, department, white paper, the police, the law.

Do not use block capitals for large amounts of text.

Use UK English: organise, modelling, fill in a form.

Use bullet points only with a lead-in line and more than one bullet. Lower case at the start of each bullet, no full stops, no semicolons, no "and" or "or" between items, one short sentence per bullet, no full stop after the last one. Numbered steps end with full stops because each is a complete sentence.

Use bold only to mark interface elements the user must act on ("Select **Start**"). To emphasise, front-load the sentence or use a heading instead.

Do not use italics. Use 'single quotes' for unusual terms (first mention only), publication titles, button names and notification names. Use "double quotes" only for direct speech.

Do not use semicolons — split into two sentences. Use round brackets, not square. Avoid "(s)" for optional plurals — use the plural.

One space after a full stop.

## Numbers, dates, times, money

Spell out "one" to "nine" except in steps, lists or where numerals make more sense. Use numerals from 10 onward. Spell out a number that starts a sentence. Use commas above 999 (9,000). Use "0.5", not ".5".

Use "to" in ranges, never hyphens or dashes: "5 to 9", "10am to 11am", "2011 to 2012". Spell out fractions with hyphens: two-thirds.

Dates: 4 June 2017 (no comma). Months upper case; can truncate to Jan, Feb where space is tight.

Times: 5:30pm, midday, midnight (or 11:59pm to remove ambiguity). Add "UK time" if relevant.

Money: £75, £75.50 (no .00), £138 million, £2 billion. Do not abbreviate to "m" or "b". Currencies are lower case.

Measurements: spell out at first mention; no space between numeral and unit (3,500kg). Celsius for temperature: 37°C.

Telephone numbers: label with "Telephone:" or "Mobile phone:". Group digits readably: 020 7946 0457, 0800 80 70 60.

Addresses: each part on a new line, no commas at line ends, country last (only if needed).

## Web writing

Readers scan, so:

- Put the most important point first.
- Use plenty of clear headings and sub-headings.
- Keep sentences and paragraphs short, one point per paragraph.
- Front-load link text with the relevant terms; never link the whole sentence.
- Do not write FAQs — start from user needs and write proper headings instead.

Page titles: 65 characters or fewer, unique, descriptive, front-loaded for search, no questions, no full stops, no dashes or slashes. Summaries: 160 characters or fewer, end with a full stop, do not repeat the title.

Use "sign in", not "log in" or "login". Use "select", not "click". Use "page", not "screen".

## Email

Be friendly, short and clear. State the purpose in the subject line. Follow normal spelling, grammar and punctuation. Only CC people who need to know.

## Editing checklist

- Read it aloud. Long, breathless sentences need splitting.
- Cut every word that does not earn its place.
- Proofread on paper if possible — you catch about 15% more errors than on screen.
- Hand the final read to someone who has not seen the draft.
